U.S. aerospace firms pursued gravity control from the 1950s

Claimed by Aviation editor Ansel Talbert (1955), later defense journalist Nick Cook · 1955 · New York Herald-Tribune series, November 1955; Cook's The Hunt for Zero Point, 2001

Partly confirmed · Some of it is on the record; the rest is not.

The claim

Talbert reported that major aircraft and electronics firms, including Glenn L. Martin, Convair, Bell, Sikorsky, Lear and Sperry, had scientists working on gravity control, that Martin had hired German physicists Burkhard Heim and Pascual Jordan, and that many scientists believed gravity could be overcome in comparatively few years. Cook later argued that the United States went on to pursue antigravity aircraft in secret. 1,2,3

The official record

Parts are on the record: Martin did contract Heim in 1955 for a gravity propulsion project and set up a research institute, and private donors funded gravity research centers. But the research that followed was mostly theoretical general relativity, and Talbert himself noted no one had yet altered gravity at all. Later claims of antigravity effects, from Podkletnov's superconductor disc to Ning Li's work, have not been reproduced, and NASA's Breakthrough Propulsion Physics Project found no evidence of antigravity-like forces. No evidence of a working gravity-control craft has emerged. 1,4,5
